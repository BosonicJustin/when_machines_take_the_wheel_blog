Part I · How we got here

Everyone asked the same question

Since ChatGPT launched in late 2022, business leaders have been asking one question: what can AI do for us? The first answers were deliberately low-risk: summarize this document, draft this email, produce a first version we'll edit. Anything wrong got caught by the human still in the loop.

It worked, so the ambition grew. AI moved into higher-value cognitive work: writing production software, drafting designs, handling support conversations. By 2025 the most ambitious teams were assembling what amounts to a company brain: systems connected to internal knowledge, data, and tools that could coordinate work and, eventually, operate parts of the business on their own.

Almost every company now uses AI somewhere. Which makes the next fact strange: the results could hardly be more unequal.

Part II · The scoreboard

The gains were real, and wildly uneven

Data from corporate expense platform Ramp puts numbers on the divide: among businesses on its platform, the top quartile of AI spenders saw revenue more than double between November 2022 and December 2025, while businesses with zero AI expenditure grew about 15%1.

Same models, same APIs, same four years. Why did some companies compound while others stalled? Talk to the teams involved and read the post-mortems, and the failures keep reducing to three problems:

Problem 01 · Unit economics

Costs that outrun the budget

Uber reportedly burned its annual engineering AI budget in four months; Microsoft cancelled most of its Claude licenses to rein in spiralling spend2. Teams now triage which workflows justify which model sizes, but on many tasks the output doesn't cover the token bill, and frontier prices are widely expected to rise as labs stop subsidizing inference from their fundraising rounds.

Problem 02 · Judgment transfer

Teaching the model how your company works

Prompt engineering and context-stuffing can embed company judgment: which tool is authoritative, what counts as sufficient evidence, when to escalate. It helps, but it caps out: for high-volume tasks with low value per call, or context-heavy multi-step work, the extra retrieval and longer prompts make each call slower and more expensive, often uneconomical to automate at all.

Problem 03 · Measurement

Proving the gains exist

Most deployments can't answer the basic question: is the AI actually right, how often, and at what cost per decision? Without a scored evaluation on your own data, "it seems to work" is the ceiling of what you can claim, and the floor of what you can improve.

Part III · The other side of the divide

What the winners do differently

The companies pulling ahead stopped treating AI as a vendor API and started treating it as a capability they own. Concretely: they teach models to operate inside their environment (their tools, their schemas, their policies, their definition of a mistake) and they measure everything against a score they trust.

The technique they've converged on is fine-tuning open-source models with reinforcement learning. Instead of describing the job in an ever-longer prompt, you place a model inside a simulated version of the workflow, let it act, score the outcomes, and train on the scores. Company judgment moves out of the prompt and into the weights.

None of this is theoretical. Over the past two years the approach has hardened into a playbook (open 4–235B base model, proprietary task data, a reinforcement-learning stage against a scored version of the workflow) and the best-documented deployments now read like a who's-who. Three are worth knowing in detail.

Bridgewater Associates, working with Thinking Machines Lab, trained an open Qwen3-235B on six financial document-judgment tasks: the kind of relevancy and boilerplate calls its analysts make all day. Carefully prompted frontier models (GPT, Claude, Gemini) topped out at 78.2%; naive prompting barely cleared 50%. The trained model reached 84.7%, roughly 30% fewer errors, at 13.8× cheaper inference3.

Harvey, the legal-AI company, ran full-parameter RL on an open 754B model for long-horizon legal agent work. Rubric pass rates rose from 85.3% to 91.3%, beating Claude Opus 4.8 Max and GPT-5.5 at maximum reasoning effort. Their stated lesson is the one this article is built around: the model only learns as well as the environment allows. The scored workflow, not the training run, is the moat.

Intercom post-trained a vertical support model, Fin Apex, on billions of customer-service interactions. It beats GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.5 on resolution rate. One customer went from 68% to 75% of issues resolved, and it runs cheaper than any frontier model, across two million issues a week.

One honest caveat before we go further, because it sharpens the claim rather than weakening it: in nearly every case above, the baseline is a prompted, un-tuned frontier model: nobody gets to fine-tune GPT-5.5's weights. The precise finding across the corpus is that task-specific post-training beats prompting the frontier on narrow, high-volume workflows. And the wins come in two economic shapes: quality wins (Bridgewater, Harvey, Intercom: beat the frontier on the metric that matters) and parity wins (LinkedIn, Instacart: match the frontier at 10–100× lower cost and latency). For most businesses, the second is where the money is.

The base model already knows how to drive. Fine-tuning teaches it your route.

That's the pattern, with the receipts. To show precisely how it works, and to check the claim rather than repeat it, we built one of these workflows end-to-end and put real numbers on every step.

Part IV

Case Study: Catalogue Integrity Agent

One of the central challenges of running an e-commerce platform is keeping the product catalog trustworthy. Every listing must land in the correct category of the platform's taxonomy, and the attributes that power search, filters, recommendations, and downstream operations must be accurately extracted from its images and description.

This work is usually done by catalog-integrity analysts, and it scales badly. As the catalog grows, so do the products, categories, attributes, and ambiguous edge cases. Inconsistent decisions propagate: products become harder to find, recommendations deteriorate, and policy violations slip through. Miss a counterfeit and you expose customers and brands to fraud; over-flag and you build an expensive review queue that frustrates legitimate sellers.

Start with the sheer scale of the asset being maintained. eBay carries about 2.5 billion live listings, Instacart's catalog spans 1.4 billion items across 1,500 retailers, and Walmart.com lists over 420 million products, ~95% of them from marketplace sellers7. Shopify's catalog absorbs more than 10 million product updates a day5. Walmart has said generative AI created or improved over 850 million of its product-catalog data points, work its CEO estimated would otherwise have taken roughly 100× the current headcount8.

And the cost of getting it wrong lands directly on revenue: in consumer surveys, 71% of shoppers say they have returned a product because it didn't match its listing, and a separate survey found 54% returning purchases over inaccurate product information, with 63% saying a bad product-information experience would push them off a trusted brand9. Bad catalog data is not a cosmetic problem; it is returns, abandoned carts, and churn.

Now the unit economics. A mid-size marketplace sees on the order of 10 million listing create-and-edit events a day. Each needs roughly three model passes (categorize, extract, check policy) over about 5,000 multimodal tokens, which is ~30 million calls a day. At frontier multimodal pricing that is roughly $1.5M a day, half a billion dollars a year, just for catalog hygiene. A fine-tuned specialist at 1/40th to 1/50th the unit cost does the same job for around $10M:

We built an AI agent that performs this workflow. For each listing it examines the image and text, searches a taxonomy of ~13,000 categories with search_taxonomy, checks the brand registry with lookup_brand, retrieves the accepted fields for its chosen category with get_attribute_schema, and then commits three structured outputs: a category, extracted attributes, and a policy verdict. When the evidence is thin or the risk is high, the case escalates to human review.

Why fine-tune an open model for this?

You could hire more analysts, but the cost grows with the catalog. You could write rules, but maintaining thousands of rules across shifting categories becomes its own engineering problem. You could call a frontier model per listing, but at ingestion scale the per-call price and latency are the whole story, and the long instruction prompt rides along with every single request.

The workflow has every marker of the case where fine-tuning wins: it happens at high volume; inputs and outputs are stable; decisions must be fast; correctness can be scored; different mistakes carry different costs; the model must use company-specific tools and schemas; and edge cases can route to humans, whose reviewed decisions become tomorrow's training data. That is why Amazon, Walmart, eBay, and Shopify have all invested heavily in custom catalog AI: at their scale, small gains in accuracy or cost per listing compound into major business value.

Part V · The experiment

A workflow the model can practice

We built the training environment from the Amazon Berkeley Objects dataset of real product listings and images, generating 177,767 catalog-review tasks. Each task is one listing. We also generated controlled policy cases (swapped images, conflicting brand claims, and deliberately legitimate brand claims as hard negatives) so every correct answer is known by construction and every decision can be scored.

The score encodes business priorities directly: category placement earns hierarchical partial credit, attributes are checked against evidence recoverable from the listing, and the policy head is asymmetric: missing a real violation costs 7× more than a false alarm, because that reflects the real-world cost structure. Unnecessary tool calls carry a small penalty.

R = 0.3·category + 0.3·attributes + 0.4·policy − tool_overage

One consequence worth understanding before reading any chart: a perfect run on this episode mix scores 0.717, not 1.0, because correctly passing a clean listing intentionally earns only partial credit. Results below are shown as a percentage of that achievable ceiling.

Step one: stop the benchmark from lying

Our first version produced impressive numbers that we did not trust, and repeated audits showed why. The attribute ground truth was effectively visible in the model's prompt, so a model could score well by copying instead of extracting. Counterfeit detection had collapsed into a lookup over twelve protected brands. Nearly 30% of image-mismatch cases reused images whose products appeared in another data split. And because most listings are clean, a lazy policy of never flagging anything was dangerously competitive. Any one of these would have produced an impressive but false model improvement. We fixed them all, locked the reward implementations byte for byte, and only then started measuring. The valuable part of custom AI is rarely pressing "train"; it is building an evaluation that cannot be cheated.

Then we gave the frontier its best shot

Before training anything, we evaluated four frontier models (GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.8, and Claude Fable 5) on 200 stratified validation episodes with identical tools, images, scorer, and turn budget. Then we tested each again with a carefully written 2,800-character workflow guide: extraction conventions, lookup procedure, three worked examples, no benchmark answers.

Part VI · Results

No prompt broke the plateau. Training did.

Eight frontier configurations landed between 0.5176 and 0.5512, a spread of 0.034 across three providers, two prompt strategies, and two Anthropic model generations. Every model found every planted violation; none was close to the ceiling. The shared gap was attribute extraction: not intelligence, but local conventions: exactly which fields to emit, what counts as a supported value, when to leave a field empty, when to stop looking.

We checked whether more thinking could close the gap. GPT-5.5-pro at maximum reasoning budget scored 0.504, statistically indistinguishable from standard GPT-5.5 at 0.500, and the newer GPT-5.6-sol at its highest reasoning effort reached 0.512. You cannot buy your way past this plateau with reasoning tokens or model recency. It is an operating-procedure ceiling, not a capability ceiling.

Then we fine-tuned Qwen3.5-9B, an open model, with a lightweight LoRA adapter using GRPO, letting it practice the workflow against the score. The setup, for those who want the receipts:

Part VII · What this buys you

From adoption to ownership

A benchmark win can be an artifact, so we checked the number three ways before believing it. The trained score comes from the same harness, episodes, tools, and scorer as every frontier run. The harness itself is insensitive: Claude Fable 5 scores 0.544 in both of our two independent harnesses. And 0.626 is the model's floor, not its ceiling: the shared harness renders the trained model through a generic chat client while the frontier models use their native tool-calling, and in its own serving path the model evaluates at about 0.67. A held-out test split remains untouched for final publication.

The score is only half the argument. The fine-tuned specialist is an asset the company owns: it runs on infrastructure you choose, so customer data and decision traces never leave your perimeter. Its behavior is pinned: no silent regressions when a provider updates a general-purpose model. Its unit cost is a rounding error next to frontier pricing and keeps falling with batching, quantization, and hardware choices you control. And every reviewed mistake becomes new evaluation and training data, a compounding loop your competitors can't copy by calling the same API you used to call.

Ownership also changes what is possible, not just what is cheap. A model you own can run continuously at high frequency, sit inside latency budgets an API round-trip cannot meet, and gate decisions before they ship instead of auditing them after. Your reward design, labeled edge cases, and decision traces stay yours: sending a proprietary workflow through a frontier API means your hardest-won judgment flows to the vendor, while a specialist keeps that alpha in-house and satisfies data-residency requirements by construction. And in multi-step workflows, per-step accuracy compounds: a five-step chain at 90% per step completes correctly 59% of the time; at 97% per step it completes 86% of the time. Raising the number on the steps that matter is where specialists earn their keep. The honest trade-off: specialization narrows. The trained 9B got better at this workflow, not at everything, which is exactly the point, and exactly why edge cases still route to humans or a frontier model.

The arithmetic of that unit cost deserves its own picture. Owning a specialist means paying once to build it (the environment, the evaluation, the training run) and then serving it at commodity rates; renting means a per-call price that never stops. At the qualification-floor volume of 45,000 decisions a day, the two curves cross in about a month:

Honesty requires the other half: fine-tuning is not the right default. If the problem is access to changing facts, use retrieval. If the task is rare and open-ended, use a frontier model. If prompting already hits the quality bar at acceptable cost, keep the prompt. And if you can't build a scored evaluation of the workflow, you are not ready to train: the evaluation, not the training run, is most of the work and most of the value.

Your workflow is a fine-tuning candidate if…

  • It happens at high volume
  • Inputs and required outputs are stable
  • Decisions must be made quickly
  • Correctness can be scored (the task is verifiable)
  • Different mistakes have different costs
  • The model must use company-specific tools and schemas
  • Edge cases can route to humans or frontier models
  • Reviewed mistakes can become new training data

Two signals alone usually settle it: sustained volume above roughly 45,000 calls a day, or a latency floor an API round-trip cannot meet. Checking six or more boxes? That is exactly the situation the audit below is built to price.

A quick way to think it through for your own stack. What data do you already have: resolved tickets, reviewed decisions, logs of the workflow being done right? Where does the judgment live: could your subject-matter experts write down what "correct" means, even roughly? How often does the workflow actually run? And the decisive question: can you attach a numerical score to an outcome, or at least a rubric a grader could apply consistently? If yes, the workflow can be practiced by a model. If you're not sure, that is precisely what an audit settles first.
