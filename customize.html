Part I · How we got here

Everyone asked the same question

Since ChatGPT launched in 2022, business leaders have been asking the same question: what can AI do for us? The answer began with low-risk tasks: summarizing documents, drafting emails, producing first drafts that a human would edit.

It quickly moved into higher-value cognitive work, such as software development and content generation, and grew into more ambitious projects: attempts to build an AI "company brain", a system connected to internal knowledge, data, and tools that could coordinate work and eventually operate parts of the business autonomously.

While a lot of time, energy and tokens have been invested in AI adoption, mesurable outcomes have barely been achieved at scale. However, some companies embraced being AI-first and saw enormous gains in productivity, revenue, and cost, while others lagged behind or failed to change their organizations enough to reach high ROI.

Recent data from corporate expense management platform Ramp reveals a stark contrast in performance: the top quartile of companies investing in AI saw their revenue more than double between November 2022 and December 2025, while businesses with zero AI expenditure experienced a mere 15% increase1.

There are many reasons why AI has done wonders for some companies and made life hard for others, but it mostly comes down to five points:

01

Redesign the process, not just the task

Becoming AI-first means rethinking how the work is structured, not dropping a model into a workflow built around people: what gets approved, who reviews what, and which handoffs still need a human. Where the process stays untouched, legacy bottlenecks absorb the productivity gains before they reach the P&L. In McKinsey's 2025 survey of organizations using gen AI, workflow redesign was the attribute most correlated with EBIT impact, and only 21% of them had redesigned any workflow at all.

02

Incentivize experimentation

Models, tooling and best practices change weekly, so last quarter's setup is rarely still the right one. That only gets picked up if people are rewarded for trying things and reporting what failed, not just for shipping. Technical teams are the natural place to start, since they see the same problems recur across functions and can tell which of them a model can actually take over.

03

Provide tailored business context

Prompt engineering and retrieval can inject business context at call time, but doing it well is its own engineering program: getting to the data, enforcing access controls on what each request may see, building retrieval that surfaces the right evidence, and managing a context window that models use unevenly as it grows.

04

Measure usage and impact

Every AI line item eventually meets the CFO question: what did this change, and was it worth it? Most deployments can't answer it, and self-reports run hot: METR found people overestimate AI's time savings by about 40 percentage points. Without a scored evaluation on your own data, "it seems to work" is the ceiling of what you can claim, and a hard budget to defend.

05

Set clear business goals

Uber blew its annual engineering budget in four months; Microsoft cancelled most of its Claude licenses to rein in spiralling costs2. Spend holds up when every workflow carries a goal and a model sized to match it, and it slips when teams buy frontier capability for tasks that never needed it. The pressure runs one way: most AI labs are currently subsidizing the token costs to capture market share.

In this article we give a detailed overview of the AI deployment technique that the companies winning with AI are rushing to adopt, fine-tuning open-source models via reinforcement learning, and show how it tackles the last three challenges above.

Part II · The other side of the divide

What the winners do differently

The companies pulling ahead stopped treating AI as a vendor API and started treating it as a capability they own. Concretely: they teach models to operate inside their environment (their tools, their schemas, their policies, their definition of a mistake) and they measure everything against a score they trust.

For specific, repeatable, well-defined workflows, their answer is fine-tuning open-source models: distilling general intelligence into specialized performance, for maximum control and cost efficiency. Organizational expertise moves directly into the model weights instead of riding along as context on every call. Frontier models keep the open-ended, novel work; the specialist takes the high-volume routine. In practice you place a model inside a simulated version of the workflow, let it act, score the outcomes, and train on the scores.

Over the past two years this has hardened into a playbook: an open base model, proprietary task data, and a reinforcement-learning stage against a scored version of the workflow. Three deployments show how it works in practice.

Bridgewater Associates, working with Thinking Machines Lab, trained an open source model (Qwen3-235B) on six financial document-judgment tasks: the kind of relevancy and boilerplate calls its analysts make all day. Carefully prompted frontier models (GPT, Claude, Gemini) topped out at 78.2%; naive prompting barely cleared 50%. The trained model reached 84.7%, roughly 30% fewer errors, at 13.8× cheaper inference3.

Harvey, the legal-AI company, ran full-parameter RL on an open 754B model for long-horizon legal agent work. Rubric pass rates rose from 85.3% to 91.3%, beating Claude Opus 4.8 Max and GPT-5.5 at maximum reasoning effort.

Intercom post-trained a vertical support model, Fin Apex, on billions of customer-service interactions. It beats GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.5 on resolution rate. One customer's resolution rate rose from 68% to 75%; the model handles roughly two million issues a week at a lower cost per issue than the frontier alternatives.

Beyond the flagships, the same shape repeats across industries:

One deployment pattern repeats across these cases: reinforcement learning pushes an open-source model past the frontier on a specific set of workflows, at a fixed and dramatically lower cost per call. The deployment typically starts by using prompt and context engineered frontier models to establish the strongest default baseline. Those frontier traces and learnings are then reused to pack the focused capability into a compact model the company owns.

In the next section we run this playbook ourselves, on one of e-commerce's most essential workflows, show you the numbers, and discuss the business gains achieved from the process.

Part III

Case Study: Catalogue Integrity Agent

One of the central challenges of running an e-commerce platform is keeping the product catalog trustworthy. Every listing must land in the correct category of the platform's taxonomy, and the attributes that power search, filters, recommendations, and downstream operations must be accurately extracted from its images and description.

+15%our fine-tuned 9B open source model reviews e-commerce product listings more accurately than the best frontier model we tested
40-340×cheaper per listing when our fine-tuned model does the reviewing instead of a frontier API

This work is usually done by catalog-integrity analysts, and the number of people scales with the catalogue size. As the catalog grows, so do the products, categories, attributes, and ambiguous edge cases. Inconsistent decisions propagate: products become harder to find, recommendations deteriorate, and policy violations slip through. Miss a counterfeit and you expose customers and brands to fraud; over-flag and you build an expensive review queue that frustrates legitimate sellers.

Start with the sheer scale of the asset being maintained. eBay carries about 2.5 billion live listings, Instacart's catalog spans 1.4 billion items across 1,500 retailers, and Walmart.com lists over 420 million products, ~95% of them from marketplace sellers7. Shopify's catalog absorbs more than 10 million product updates a day5. Walmart has said generative AI created or improved over 850 million of its product-catalog data points, work its CEO estimated would otherwise have taken roughly 100× the current headcount8.

And the cost of getting it wrong lands directly on revenue: in consumer surveys, 71% of shoppers say they have returned a product because it didn't match its listing, and a separate survey found 54% returning purchases over inaccurate product information, with 63% saying a bad product-information experience would push them off a trusted brand9. Bad catalog data is not a cosmetic problem; it is returns, abandoned carts, and churn.

Now the unit economics. A mid-size marketplace sees on the order of 10 million listing create-and-edit events a day. Each needs roughly three model passes (categorize, extract, check policy) over about 5,000 multimodal tokens, which is ~30 million calls a day. At frontier multimodal pricing that is roughly $1.5M a day, half a billion dollars a year, just for catalog hygiene. A fine-tuned specialist at 1/40th to 1/50th the unit cost does the same job for around $10M:

The companies actually running this workflow confirm the math. Shopify classifies products with fine-tuned open vision-language models at roughly 40 million multimodal inferences a day, a volume it says commercial APIs can't economically serve, and separately replaced a closed frontier model in its Flow agent with a fine-tuned model that is 2.2× faster and 68% cheaper5. Instacart's fine-tuned 8B query-understanding model matches frontier F1 (95.7 vs 95.8) at 300 ms instead of 700, and halved tail complaints. DoorDash trained a menu-ingestion agent with RL against an automated grader and cut critical menu errors 30%, now rolled out US-wide. Taobao runs GRPO-trained retrieval in production search at China's largest marketplace; Amazon built Rufus as a custom shopping model precisely because frontier APIs don't pencil at its scale. And Ramp, the same company whose spending data opened this article, built a custom spreadsheet-search agent that exceeded Claude Opus 4.6 with a 4% accuracy gain and 27% faster responses, at the pricing and speed of much smaller models4.

We built an AI agent to perform this workflow. The agent examines the listing, searches the product taxonomy, checks brand information, retrieves the appropriate attribute schema, and produces a structured decision. When the available evidence is insufficient or the risk is too high, the case is escalated for human review.

Why fine-tune an open source model for this?

One simple solution would be to hire human analysts to audit the catalog, but operating costs grow with the catalog. Rules-based software is cheaper, but maintaining thousands of rules across changing categories and products quickly becomes difficult. Calling a frontier model per listing works, but at ingestion scale the per-call price and latency are the whole story.

Fine-tuning an open-source model turns that repeatable workflow into a specialist the company controls. It requires an upfront investment, but delivers lower costs per listing, faster decisions, more consistent behavior, and less dependence on an external AI provider. That is why e-commerce giants such as Shopify, Amazon, Walmart, and eBay have invested heavily in custom catalog AI: at larger scale, even small improvements in accuracy or cost per listing compound into major business value.

Part IV · The experiment

A workflow the model can practice

We built the training environment from the Amazon Berkeley Objects dataset, which contains real product images and listing information. From this data we created 177,767 catalog-review tasks. Each task contains a product image, title, description, claimed brand, and region. We also created controlled policy cases, including mismatched images, conflicting brand claims, and deliberately legitimate brand claims as hard negatives. Because these cases were generated deliberately, we knew the correct answer and could measure the model's performance.

The model could use three company-like tools: search a taxonomy of approximately 13,000 categories, check whether a brand is registered and protected, and retrieve the required attributes for a selected category. It then had to return the product category, the extracted attributes, and a policy decision. The score rewarded correct outputs and penalized missed violations, unsupported attributes, invalid categories, and unnecessary tool calls: this let us encode business priorities directly into the training process, with a missed violation costing 7× more than a false alarm.

R = 0.3·category + 0.3·attributes + 0.4·policy − tool_overage

One consequence worth understanding before reading any chart: a perfect run on this episode mix scores 0.717, not 1.0, because correctly passing a clean listing intentionally earns only partial credit. Results below are shown as a percentage of that achievable ceiling.

Step one: stop the benchmark from lying

Our first version produced impressive numbers that we did not trust, and repeated audits showed why. The attribute ground truth was effectively visible in the model's prompt, so a model could score well by copying instead of extracting. Counterfeit detection had collapsed into a lookup over twelve protected brands. Nearly 30% of image-mismatch cases reused images whose products appeared in another data split. And because most listings are clean, a lazy policy of never flagging anything was dangerously competitive. Any one of these would have produced an impressive but false model improvement. We fixed them all, locked the reward implementations byte for byte, and only then started measuring. The valuable part of custom AI is rarely pressing "train"; it is building an evaluation that cannot be cheated.

Then we gave the frontier its best shot

Before training, we tested four frontier models (GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.8, and Claude Fable 5) with and without a detailed workflow guide, on 200 stratified validation episodes with identical tools, images, scorer, and turn budget. The guide held 2,800 characters of extraction conventions, lookup procedure, and worked examples, with no benchmark answers.

Part V · Results

No prompt broke the plateau. Training did.

Eight frontier configurations landed between 0.5176 and 0.5512, a spread of 0.034 across three providers, two prompt strategies, and two Anthropic model generations. Every model found every planted violation; none was close to the ceiling. The shared gap was attribute extraction: not intelligence, but local conventions: exactly which fields to emit, what counts as a supported value, when to leave a field empty, when to stop looking.

We checked whether more thinking could close the gap. GPT-5.5-pro at maximum reasoning budget scored 0.504, statistically indistinguishable from standard GPT-5.5 at 0.500, and the newer GPT-5.6-sol at its highest reasoning effort reached 0.512. You cannot buy your way past this plateau with reasoning tokens or model recency. It is an operating-procedure ceiling, not a capability ceiling.

We then fine-tuned a Qwen3.5-9B open-source model using a lightweight LoRA adapter and the GRPO reinforcement-learning framework, letting it practice the workflow against the score. The setup, for those who want the receipts:

Part VI · What this buys you

From adoption to ownership

A benchmark win can be an artifact, so we checked the number three ways before believing it. The trained score comes from the same harness, episodes, tools, and scorer as every frontier run. The harness itself is insensitive: Claude Fable 5 scores 0.544 in both of our two independent harnesses. And 0.626 is the model's floor, not its ceiling: the shared harness renders the trained model through a generic chat client while the frontier models use their native tool-calling, and in its own serving path the model evaluates at about 0.67. A held-out test split remains untouched for final publication.

The score is only half the argument. The fine-tuned specialist is an asset the company owns: it runs on infrastructure you choose, so customer data and decision traces never leave your perimeter. Its behavior is pinned: no silent regressions when a provider updates a general-purpose model. Its unit cost is a rounding error next to frontier pricing and keeps falling with batching, quantization, and hardware choices you control. And every reviewed mistake becomes new evaluation and training data, a compounding loop your competitors can't copy by calling the same API you used to call.

Ownership also changes what is possible, not just what is cheap. A model you own can run continuously at high frequency, sit inside latency budgets an API round-trip cannot meet, and gate decisions before they ship instead of auditing them after. Your reward design, labeled edge cases, and decision traces stay yours: sending a proprietary workflow through a frontier API means your hardest-won judgment flows to the vendor, while a specialist keeps that alpha in-house and satisfies data-residency requirements by construction. And in multi-step workflows, per-step accuracy compounds: a five-step chain at 90% per step completes correctly 59% of the time; at 97% per step it completes 86% of the time. Raising the number on the steps that matter is where specialists earn their keep. The honest trade-off: specialization narrows. The trained 9B got better at this workflow, not at everything, which is exactly the point, and exactly why edge cases still route to humans or a frontier model.

The arithmetic of that unit cost deserves its own picture. Owning a specialist means paying once to build it (the environment, the evaluation, the training run) and then serving it at commodity rates; renting means a per-call price that never stops. Honesty requires the other half: fine-tuning is not the right default. If the problem is access to changing facts, use retrieval. If the task is rare and open-ended, use a frontier model. If prompting already hits the quality bar at acceptable cost, keep the prompt. And if you can't build a scored evaluation of the workflow, you are not ready to train: the evaluation, not the training run, is most of the work and most of the value.

Your workflow is a fine-tuning candidate if at least one point applies…

  • It happens at high volume
  • Inputs and required outputs are stable
  • Decisions must be made quickly
  • Correctness can be scored (the task is verifiable)
  • Different mistakes have different costs
  • The model must use company-specific tools and schemas
  • Edge cases can route to humans or frontier models
  • Reviewed mistakes can become new training data
  • Sensitive data cannot leave infrastructure you control

That last one is also the safety argument: a model you train runs inside your own boundary, so prompts and records never reach a vendor, and on the task it was trained for it still matched or beat every frontier model we tested. Two of them settle it on their own: sustained volume above roughly 45,000 calls a day, or a latency floor an API round-trip cannot meet. One is enough to be worth a conversation, and working out which ones apply is what the call is for.

A quick way to think it through for your own stack. What data do you already have: resolved tickets, reviewed decisions, logs of the workflow being done right? Where does the judgment live: could your subject-matter experts write down what "correct" means, even roughly? How often does the workflow actually run? And the decisive question: can you attach a numerical score to an outcome, or at least a rubric a grader could apply consistently? If yes, the workflow can be practiced by a model. If you're not sure, that is precisely what an audit settles first.
